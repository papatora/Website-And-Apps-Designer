The case for fewer colors

Most interfaces don't have a color problem. They have a too-many-colors problem.

By Ines Takahashi18 September 202611 min read
Contents
  1. Where the extra colors come from
  2. A method: one accent, earned
  3. What neutrals are for

Open any product built in the last five years and count the colors that mean something. Not the ones that are there — the ones that mean something. In most interfaces the first number is somewhere around twenty and the second is about three. Everything in between is noise wearing a brand guideline.

This isn't an argument for grey. It's an argument that color is a signal, and a signal that fires constantly stops being heard.

Where the extra colors come from

They arrive one reasonable decision at a time. Marketing wants the pricing badge to “pop”. A status needs a fourth state, so it gets a fourth hue. Someone adds a gradient to the hero because the page “felt flat”, and now there are two blues that almost match.1

A signal that fires constantly stops being heard.

None of these is wrong on its own. The problem is cumulative: every new color lowers the value of every existing one.

A method: one accent, earned

Start with neutrals only — a background, a surface, two inks and a line. Build the entire screen. Then ask what the one action is that a person should take here, and give that, and only that, the accent.

If a second thing needs emphasis, try weight, size or position before reaching for hue. Status colors are the exception, because they carry meaning outside your brand: danger is red in every product your users have ever touched.

What neutrals are for

Neutrals do the structural work — hierarchy, grouping, depth — so the accent doesn't have to. Tint them slightly toward your accent's hue and the page feels designed rather than default, without adding a single new color.2

  1. In one audit of a mid-size SaaS product we found 14 distinct blues in production CSS, 11 of them introduced by accident. ↩
  2. A 2–4% chroma shift is usually enough. More than that and your “neutral” becomes a color. ↩
